la grande idée

1. Introduction (Purpose: orient the listener)

  • Present the context of the passage (historical, literary).

  • State the dominant theme or tension the sermon will address.

  • Give the sermon’s main idea (the “Big Idea / Question” or “Proposition”).

2. Exposition of the Text (Verse-by-verse or section-by-section)

This is the backbone. Each movement explains what the text says and means.

Usually structured as:

Point 1 — What the text says (explanation) Explain key words, phrases, and ideas. Show how this part fits the passage’s flow.

Point 2 — Why it matters (theological significance) Connect the text to biblical theology. Show what God is revealing about Himself, humanity, or salvation.

Point 3 — How it applies (implication for life) Concrete, specific application. Often includes examples, warnings, encouragements.

Each point follows the same mini‑pattern: Explanation → Illustration → Application

🧩 3. Illustrations (woven throughout)

Clarify difficult concepts.

Make abstract truths concrete.

Should illuminate the text, not distract from it.

🎯 4. Application (specific, actionable, text‑rooted)

Application can be:

Personal (heart, habits, character)

Communal (church life)

Missional (witness, service)

Ethical (choices, behavior)

Good expository preaching ties each application directly to the text, not to general moral advice.

5. Conclusion

Restate the main idea.

Summarize the key movements of the text.

Call for a response (faith, obedience, trust, repentance).

End with prayer or reflection.

🧱 Expository Sermon Skeleton — Psalm 44 Theme: When God feels absent, faith remembers, laments, and pleads.

Big Idea: God’s past faithfulness becomes the foundation for honest lament and bold prayer in present suffering.

I. Remembering God’s Past Faithfulness (vv. 1–8) Explanation:
Israel recalls what God did for their ancestors—victories, deliverance, covenant love. Their confidence was not in themselves but in God’s power.

Illustration:
A family photo album: remembering who you are by remembering where you came from.

Application:

Faith begins with remembering.

Rehearse God’s past works in Scripture and in your own life.

Gratitude strengthens trust before hardship arrives.

  1. Lamenting Present Confusion and Pain (vv. 9–16) Explanation:
    The psalm shifts sharply: despite their faithfulness, they feel abandoned. They suffer defeat, humiliation, and shame. This is not rebellion—it is honest lament.

Illustration:
A believer who keeps serving faithfully yet faces unexpected loss.

Application:

Scripture gives permission to say, “God, this doesn’t make sense.”

Lament is not unbelief; it is faith refusing to pretend.

Bring your confusion to God instead of burying it.

  1. Affirming Covenant Loyalty Despite Suffering (vv. 17–22) Explanation:
    Israel insists they have not turned away from God. Their suffering is not due to sin. Verse 22 becomes a key text later quoted by Paul (Rom 8:36).

Illustration:
A soldier who remains loyal even when the battle seems unwinnable.

Application:

Obedience is not a guarantee of ease.

Faithfulness matters even when God feels silent.

Suffering can be part of God’s mysterious purposes.

  1. Pleading Boldly for God’s Intervention (vv. 23–26) Explanation:
    The psalm ends with a cry: “Awake… Rise up… Redeem us.”
    This is bold, urgent prayer grounded in God’s steadfast love.

Illustration:
A child calling out in the night—not because they doubt the parent’s love, but because they trust it.

Application:

Pray boldly when God feels distant.

Anchor your pleas in God’s character, not your performance.

Hope persists even when circumstances contradict it.

Conclusion Remember God’s past works.

Lament honestly before Him.

Remain faithful even in confusion.

Cry out boldly for His help. Psalm 44 teaches that mature faith is not silent, stoic, or simplistic—it remembers, wrestles, and pleads.
